# Decolonial AI, Technological Dependency, and Alternatives — Field Map

Status: working research map

Purpose: This file is a high-level map of the intellectual field for future Thesisound research. It is intentionally **not** an episode plan or a reading list yet. The next step is to refine the taxonomy, identify overlaps and omissions, and only then drill down into sources and possible episodes.

## Core research question

How should we understand the concentration of power and dependency around modern AI — across chips, compute/cloud, foundation models, data, labor, platforms, and distribution — through the lenses of coloniality, political economy, technological dependency, sovereignty, geopolitics, and sociotechnical alternatives?

The aim is not only to critique Big Tech or capitalism in general. A central concern is how asymmetric technological dependence can become political and geopolitical power, especially for countries and communities outside the main centers of AI infrastructure.

---

## 1. Coloniality, postcolonialism, and technology

**Central question:** How do colonial relations of power, knowledge, and hierarchy persist through technological systems even after formal colonial rule?

**Keywords**
- decoloniality
- coloniality of power
- coloniality of knowledge
- postcolonial technology
- decolonial computing
- decolonial AI

**Role in the map:** Primarily a theoretical lens. It provides vocabulary for understanding continuity between older colonial structures and contemporary technological systems, but does not by itself provide a complete account of AI infrastructure or practical alternatives.

**Anchor:** Mohamed, Png & Isaac, *Decolonial AI* (2020).

---

## 2. Digital colonialism, data colonialism, and extractivism

**Central question:** How are resources, data, labor, attention, and knowledge extracted from peripheral populations and converted into value elsewhere?

**Keywords**
- digital colonialism
- data colonialism
- data extractivism
- digital extractivism
- digital labor
- platform capitalism

**Role in the map:** Connects colonial analysis to contemporary digital capitalism and helps describe center–periphery relations in data-intensive systems.

**Anchors:** Nick Couldry & Ulises A. Mejias; Tim Nothias.

---

## 3. Political economy and the material infrastructure of AI

**Central question:** What material and economic systems make AI possible, who owns them, and where is power concentrated?

**Analytical stack**

`chips → compute → cloud → models → data → labor → applications/distribution`

**Keywords**
- political economy of AI
- AI infrastructure
- infrastructural power
- intellectual monopoly
- cloud concentration
- AI supply chains
- compute governance
- materiality of AI

**Role in the map:** Corrects the tendency to treat AI as immaterial software. It brings semiconductors, energy, data centers, labor, capital, and ownership into the analysis.

**Anchor:** Kate Crawford, *Atlas of AI*.

---

## 4. Technological dependency, sovereignty, and strategic autonomy

**Central question:** When does technological dependence become structural political dependence, and which dependencies should states or societies attempt to reduce?

**Keywords**
- technological sovereignty
- digital sovereignty
- AI sovereignty
- strategic autonomy
- technological dependency
- cloud sovereignty
- semiconductor sovereignty
- techno-blocs

**Role in the map:** Closest to the practical geopolitical question motivating this research. It shifts the discussion from critique of firms toward capabilities, dependencies, resilience, autonomy, and state strategy.

**Important caution:** Sovereignty should not automatically mean autarky, national self-sufficiency, or state control. The concept itself has competing state-centric, individual, community, corporate, network, and commons-based interpretations.

---

## 5. Weaponized interdependence and geopolitical coercion

**Central question:** How can control over central nodes in global technological networks be turned into coercive geopolitical power?

**Keywords**
- weaponized interdependence
- chokepoint power
- network power
- economic statecraft
- export controls
- semiconductor geopolitics
- sanctions and technology

**Role in the map:** Explains why dependency is not merely an economic-efficiency question. A network can be mutually beneficial in normal conditions while becoming an instrument of coercion during geopolitical conflict.

**Anchor:** Henry Farrell & Abraham Newman, *Weaponized Interdependence*.

---

## 6. Epistemic, linguistic, and cultural coloniality in AI

**Central question:** Which languages, forms of knowledge, cultures, values, categories, and worldviews become legible to AI systems, and who defines the standards by which systems are trained and evaluated?

**Keywords**
- epistemic coloniality
- epistemic injustice
- linguistic colonialism
- low-resource languages
- indigenous knowledge and AI
- decolonial AI ethics
- algorithmic bias
- benchmark bias

**Role in the map:** Prevents the analysis from collapsing into infrastructure ownership alone. Dependency may also be reproduced through datasets, benchmarks, taxonomies, language coverage, and definitions of valid knowledge.

---

## 7. Data sovereignty, community governance, and commons

**Central question:** Who should have authority over data and the conditions under which it is collected, shared, processed, and used to train AI?

**Keywords**
- data sovereignty
- indigenous data sovereignty
- data governance
- data stewardship
- data commons
- data localization
- community data governance

**Role in the map:** A bridge between diagnosis and institutional design. It introduces alternatives to both unrestricted corporate extraction and simplistic state ownership.

---

## 8. Sociotechnical alternatives and public infrastructure

**Central question:** What combinations of technology, institutions, ownership models, and governance could reduce concentration and dependency without requiring every actor to reproduce the entire AI stack independently?

**Keywords**
- public compute
- computing commons
- public AI
- digital commons
- digital public infrastructure
- open-source AI
- platform cooperativism
- data commons
- community governance

**Role in the map:** The most explicitly solution-oriented part of the field. It includes institutional and technical designs rather than purely regulatory or critical responses.

**Key issue to investigate:** Open-source models alone may not solve infrastructure dependence because training, post-training, inference, evaluation, and deployment still require compute and supporting infrastructure.

---

## 9. Big Tech, state power, military infrastructure, and war

**Central question:** Are large technology companies merely private firms regulated by states, or are they becoming components of state security and military infrastructures?

**Keywords**
- military-tech complex
- Big Tech and military
- platformization of warfare
- AI and warfare
- dual-use technology
- military cloud
- defense AI

**Role in the map:** Connects platform and infrastructure concentration to security and warfare. This is analytically distinct from general technological dependency: it concerns the integration of private technological infrastructures with military and security power.

---

# Relations among the clusters

These clusters do not form a single school of thought. The relevant field sits at the intersection of several traditions:

- Postcolonial / Decolonial Studies
- Science and Technology Studies (STS)
- Critical AI Studies
- Political Economy
- International Political Economy
- International Relations / Security Studies
- Infrastructure Studies
- AI Governance

A rough horizontal trajectory is:

`Coloniality → Digital/Data Colonialism → Material Political Economy of AI → Technological Dependency → Weaponized Dependency → Sovereignty → Sociotechnical Alternatives`

A second, vertical axis cuts across the whole map:

`knowledge / language / culture → data → labor → models → cloud / compute → chips / energy → state / military power`

The two axes should not be confused. For example, linguistic coloniality and semiconductor dependency are very different mechanisms of power even if both may be described as forms of technological asymmetry.

# Adjacent but not central literature

## Surveillance capitalism

Shoshana Zuboff's *Surveillance Capitalism* is relevant but should not currently be treated as the backbone of this research map. Its main analytical problem is the extraction of behavioral data and the resulting corporate power over users and societies. The core question here is broader and includes asymmetric relations among countries, communities, infrastructure owners, states, and geopolitical blocs.

## Platform capitalism

Useful for understanding ownership, accumulation, network effects, and platform power, but insufficient on its own for coloniality, geopolitical dependency, or sovereignty.

# Working hypothesis to test

A central hypothesis for the next research stage is:

> Decolonial and postcolonial approaches may be stronger at diagnosing extraction, epistemic hierarchy, and asymmetric power than at specifying technically and institutionally viable alternatives for the AI stack.

If this hypothesis holds, solution-oriented literature will probably have to be assembled from adjacent fields such as:

- public compute
- digital/public infrastructure
- commons governance
- industrial policy
- strategic autonomy
- open-source ecosystems
- cooperative/platform ownership
- indigenous/community data governance
- distributed or regional infrastructure

This hypothesis should be tested rather than assumed.

# Open questions for taxonomy refinement

1. Should `digital colonialism` and `data colonialism` remain one cluster or be separated?
2. Is `AI sovereignty` analytically useful, or does it conceal very different goals under one label?
3. Should labor and global AI supply chains become a separate cluster rather than remain inside political economy?
4. Should ecological/resource extraction — minerals, energy, water, data centers — become its own branch?
5. Does the current map overemphasize states and underrepresent cities, communities, regions, cooperatives, and transnational institutions?
6. Is `colonialism` always the right frame for technological dependency, or do dependency theory, world-systems theory, and international political economy sometimes explain the same phenomena more precisely?
7. Which dependencies are genuinely dangerous, and which are normal consequences of specialization and global trade?
8. What would technological sovereignty mean without falling into technological autarky?

1. challenge the taxonomy;
2. identify missing traditions and conceptual overlaps;

4. identify the strongest anchor sources for each surviving cluster.

Only after that should the map be decomposed into Thesisound episode candidates.
