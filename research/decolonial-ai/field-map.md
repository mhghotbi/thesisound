## 0. Orientation

### Core research problem

How should concentration and asymmetric dependency in contemporary AI be understood across semiconductors, compute, cloud, models, software stacks, data, labor, language, platforms, standards, and state/security infrastructures — and how do political economy, coloniality, technological capability, geopolitical coercion, sovereignty, and sociotechnical alternatives relate without being conflated?

The field does **not** form one coherent literature called `AI colonialism`. It is an intersection of several intellectual traditions that explain different mechanisms.

### The main structural rule

Do not represent this field as one tree of sibling “topics.” The map uses five linked views:

- `L` — **Literatures / intellectual traditions**: which bodies of scholarship explain the problem?
- `S` — **AI stack / empirical sites**: where in the system does the relation occur?
- `P` — **Power mechanisms**: how is power produced or exercised?
- `R` — **Responses / alternatives**: what intervention or counter-structure is proposed?
- `C` — **Case / geopolitical positions**: what role does a country, region, institution, or community occupy?

A later Thesisound research question can therefore be composed as:

`Literature (L) × Site (S) × Power mechanism (P) × Response (R) × Case position (C)`

This prevents category errors such as treating `Political Economy`, `labor`, `epistemic power`, and `public compute` as if they were the same type of object.

# 1. L — Literature / intellectual-tradition map

## L1. Political Economy of Digital / AI Capitalism

**Status:** ESTABLISHED

**Central question:** Who owns and controls critical assets, knowledge, platforms, data, infrastructure, and intellectual property; how are rents, monopoly power, vertical integration, and accumulation reproduced?

**What it explains well:**
- market and ownership concentration
- platform power
- rents and intellectual monopoly
- corporate control of data/knowledge
- vertical integration and ecosystem power
- relationships between capital concentration and innovation structures

**What it does not explain alone:**
- colonial histories and epistemic hierarchy
- material dependency at every infrastructure layer
- geopolitical coercion through state-controlled chokepoints
- legitimate forms of sovereignty or community self-determination

**Anchor sources:**
- Cecilia Rikap, *Capitalism, Power and Innovation: Intellectual Monopoly Capitalism Uncovered* (Routledge, 2021)
  - https://www.routledge.com/Capitalism-Power-and-Innovation-Intellectual-Monopoly-Capitalism-Uncovered/Rikap/p/book/9780367750299
- Hao Qi, “Platform power: monopolisation and financialisation in the era of big tech,” *Cambridge Journal of Economics* (2022)
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/cje/beac054
- Michael Kemmerling et al., “Digital power resources (DPR),” *Socio-Economic Review* (2023)
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/ser/mwac059

**Relation to other nodes:** Strongly connected to L3 Infrastructure, Data/Digital Colonialism, Digital Labor, competition policy, and the Big Tech–security nexus.

## L2. Critical Sociotechnical Studies of Data and AI

**Status:** COMPOSITE FAMILY

Internal maturity:
- **Science and Technology Studies (STS):** ESTABLISHED discipline
- **Critical Data Studies:** ESTABLISHED subfield
- **Critical AI Studies:** EMERGING field label

**Central question:** How do technical systems classify, normalize, materialize, and distribute power, and how are technologies embedded in institutions, histories, categories, practices, and forms of labor?

**What it explains well:**
- sociotechnical construction of data/AI systems
- classification and normalization
- infrastructures and practices hidden behind technical interfaces
- bias, datafication, labor, maintenance, categorization, and institutional context
- why technical design is never merely technical

**Boundary:** Critical Data Studies and STS should not be collapsed into political economy; they ask different questions even when they study the same companies or infrastructures.

**Anchor sources:**

**Relation to other nodes:** Major bridge to L3 Infrastructure, L4 Decolonial/Postcolonial technology studies, Data Colonialism, Digital Labor, language/benchmark analysis, and standards.

## L3. AI Infrastructure, Materiality & Political Ecology

**Status:** VERIFIED COMPOSITE PROBLEM FAMILY — not one bounded discipline

**Central question:** What material, organizational, ecological, and infrastructural systems make AI possible, and where do dependencies accumulate?

**What it explains well:**
- minerals and physical extraction
- energy and water use
- semiconductor and accelerator dependence
- data centers and compute
- cloud infrastructure
- logistics, maintenance, supply chains
- software/infrastructure path dependence
- the industrialization of AI

**Why separate from L1:** Ownership and accumulation are not identical to infrastructural dependence. An actor can face severe dependency even where there are multiple nominal suppliers if those suppliers share upstream hardware, standards, software stacks, or cloud interfaces.

**Anchor sources:**
- Kate Crawford & Vladan Joler, “Anatomy of an AI System” (2018)
  - https://anatomyof.ai/
- Kate Crawford, *Atlas of AI* (Yale University Press, 2021)
  - https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300264630/atlas-of-ai/

## L4. Postcolonial / Decolonial Technology Studies & Computing

**Status:** ESTABLISHED GENEALOGY; AI-SPECIFIC BRANCH IS NEWER

**Central question:** How do histories and structures of coloniality, hierarchy, development, knowledge, language, and externally imposed technological systems persist or reappear through technology?

**What it explains well:**
- coloniality of power/knowledge in technical systems
- historical continuity beyond formal colonial administration
- technology transfer and design as non-neutral
- epistemic and linguistic hierarchy
- externally imposed systems and development imaginaries
- the politics of who defines technical categories and legitimate knowledge

**Important genealogy:**
- Postcolonial Computing precedes the current AI boom.
- Decolonial AI is a newer bridge applying decolonial theory to AI sociotechnical practice.

**Anchor sources:**
- Lilly Irani, Janet Vertesi, Paul Dourish, Kavita Philip & Rebecca Grinter, “Postcolonial Computing: A Lens on Design and Development,” CHI (2010)
  - https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753522
- Kavita Philip, Lilly Irani & Paul Dourish, “Postcolonial Computing: A Tactical Survey,” *Science, Technology, & Human Values* (2012)
  - https://doi.org/10.1177/0162243910389594
- Shakir Mohamed, Marie-Therese Png & William Isaac, “Decolonial AI,” *Philosophy & Technology* (2020)
  - https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-020-00405-8

**Boundary:** This literature is not the parent explanation for the entire AI economy. It does not by itself explain semiconductor bottlenecks, market concentration, or public-infrastructure financing.

## L5. Dependency, Development & Technological Capability

**Status:** ESTABLISHED HISTORICAL LINEAGE; AI APPLICATION EMERGING

**Central question:** How are asymmetric productive and technological capabilities reproduced such that actors may use or access technology without controlling the capability to develop, substitute, maintain, or reproduce it?

**What it explains well:**
- center–periphery capability asymmetry
- dependence on machinery, capital goods, and advanced technology
- difference between access and endogenous capability
- why importing technology can coexist with structural dependence

**Anchor source:**
- Theotonio Dos Santos, “The Structure of Dependence,” *American Economic Review* 60(2), 1970
  - https://www.aeaweb.org/aer/top20/60.2.231-236.pdf

**Important status note:** There is no mature canon verified here called `AI Dependency Theory`. The historical dependency/capability literature is established; applying it systematically to compute, cloud, semiconductors, models, and software stacks is an emerging analytical bridge.

**Key distinction:**

`access to technology ≠ endogenous capability`

**Relation to other nodes:** L5 is the crucial bridge between L1/L3 diagnosis and L7 sovereignty. Without it, the map jumps too quickly from “concentration exists” to “therefore sovereignty is the answer.”

## L6. International Political Economy, Geoeconomics & Security of Technology

**Status:** ESTABLISHED

**Central question:** How can network position, supply chains, finance, standards, export controls, infrastructure, and strategic chokepoints be converted into state power or coercion?

**What it explains well:**
- weaponized interdependence
- chokepoint power
- export controls
- sanctions and technology denial
- semiconductor geopolitics
- techno-blocs and strategic alignment
- economic statecraft

**Anchor source:**
- Henry Farrell & Abraham Newman, “Weaponized Interdependence: How Global Economic Networks Shape State Coercion,” *International Security* 44(1), 2019
  - https://doi.org/10.1162/isec_a_00351

**Key distinction:**

`dependency ≠ weaponized dependency`

A dependency can exist without active coercion. Weaponized Interdependence specifies when asymmetric network position becomes usable coercive power.

**Relation to other nodes:** Strongly connected to L3 infrastructure, L5 dependency, L7 sovereignty/strategic autonomy, standards, semiconductor policy, and the military-security nexus.

## L7. Digital / Technological / Data Sovereignty & Strategic Autonomy

**Status:** ESTABLISHED BUT CONTESTED FAMILY; AI-SPECIFIC TERMS EMERGING

Internal maturity:
- **Digital sovereignty:** established, contested
- **Data sovereignty:** established, contested
- **Technological sovereignty / strategic autonomy:** established policy/IR vocabulary
- **AI sovereignty:** EMERGING and conceptually unstable
- **Compute sovereignty:** EMERGING technical-policy sub-concept

**Central question:** What meaningful authority, capability, jurisdiction, ownership, resilience, or control over digital/AI systems should exist, and for which actor?

**Do not treat sovereignty as one thing. Decompose it by:**

### Object
- data
- cloud
- compute
- models
- software stack
- semiconductors
- broader technological capability

### Subject / actor
- state
- region
- public institution
- individual
- community
- Indigenous nation/people
- commons/cooperative institution

### Mode
- jurisdiction
- ownership
- operational control
- endogenous capability
- resilience
- substitutability / exit capacity

**Important verified warning:** Sovereignty can be double-edged. Policies framed as protection from foreign corporate power can also expand localization, surveillance, censorship, or domestic state/security control.

**Anchor sources:**
- Anupam Chander & Haochen Sun (eds.), *Data Sovereignty: From the Digital Silk Road to the Return of the State* (Oxford University Press, 2023)
  - https://global.oup.com/academic/product/data-sovereignty-9780197582794
- Chander & Sun, “Digital Sovereignty as Double-Edged Sword”
  - https://academic.oup.com/book/55261/chapter/428625183

**Relation to other nodes:** Response-oriented bridge from L5/L6 to R families; must also be checked against L8 Indigenous/community sovereignty to avoid state-centrism.

## L8. Indigenous Data Sovereignty & Community Data Governance

**Status:** ESTABLISHED DISTINCT LITERATURE AND GOVERNANCE TRADITION

**Central question:** How can collective self-determination, authority, responsibility, benefit, and ethical governance over data be structured outside purely corporate or state-centric models?

**What it contributes:**
- sovereignty not reducible to the nation-state
- collective rather than purely individual data rights
- authority to control use and reuse
- governance grounded in community self-determination
- a concrete bridge from critique to institutional principles

**Anchor sources:**
- Tahu Kukutai & John Taylor (eds.), *Indigenous Data Sovereignty: Toward an Agenda* (ANU Press, 2016)
  - https://doi.org/10.22459/CAEPR38.11.2016
- Stephanie Russo Carroll et al., “The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance,” *Data Science Journal* (2020)
  - https://doi.org/10.5334/dsj-2020-043

**Important correction:** CARE should not be framed simplistically as `CARE vs FAIR`. CARE is designed to complement data-centric FAIR principles by adding people, purpose, collective benefit, authority, responsibility, and ethics.

**Boundary:** Do not collapse Indigenous Data Sovereignty into generic `data commons`, `open data`, or Creative Commons licensing. Openness can conflict with community authority.

# 4. P — Power-mechanism map

This overlay answers: **how** is power created or exercised?

## P1. Economic / Ownership / Appropriative Power

Mechanisms may include:
- monopoly / oligopoly
- rent extraction
- intellectual-property control
- vertical integration
- enclosure / appropriation
- labor exploitation
- data extraction

Primary explanatory homes: L1 Political Economy, parts of L2/L4.

## P2. Infrastructural / Dependency Power

Mechanisms may include:
- supplier dependence
- bottlenecks
- lock-in
- switching costs
- software-stack dependence
- standards dependence
- absence of endogenous capability
- dependence on critical upstream services

Primary explanatory homes: L3 Infrastructure, L5 Dependency/Capability, standards literature.

## P3a. External / Geopolitical Coercion

Mechanisms may include:
- denial of access
- export restrictions
- sanctions
- chokepoint control
- strategic exploitation of network centrality
- military/strategic supply-chain leverage

Primary explanatory home: L6 IPE/Security.

## P3b. Internal Administrative / Security Coercion

Mechanisms may include:
- surveillance
- censorship
- policing
- forced localization where it expands state access/control
- administrative control through identity/data infrastructures

Primary explanatory homes: L7 sovereignty/governance, security studies, critical data/STS.

**Important:** P3b exists to prevent a false binary in which only foreign/corporate domination counts as power. Reducing external dependence can coexist with increased domestic coercion.

## P4. Epistemic / Constitutive Power

Mechanisms may include:
- classification
- normalization
- linguistic hierarchy
- benchmark/default setting
- representation and legibility
- defining which knowledge/categories count as valid or computable

Primary explanatory homes: L2 Critical Data/STS, L4 Postcolonial/Decolonial technology studies, standards interfaces.

### Why there is no P5 “labor/material power”

Labor, minerals, energy, compute, and data are mainly resources/sites/substrates. Their political significance comes from mechanisms such as ownership, dependency, coercion, or epistemic constitution. Treating “labor” as a sibling of those mechanisms would mix ontological levels again.

# 5. R — Response / alternative families

This overlay answers: **what changes are proposed?** Technical and institutional components must be distinguished.

## R1. Regulation / Competition / Anti-Lock-In

Examples:
- antitrust
- interoperability requirements
- portability
- procurement rules
- switching rights / exit rights
- anti-bundling or anti-tying measures

Addresses primarily: P1 and P2.

## R2. Public Provision / Public Infrastructure

Examples:
- Public Compute
- shared public GPU/HPC capacity
- public/regional cloud
- Public AI services/models where genuinely publicly governed

**Status:** Public Compute and Public AI are emerging real policy families.

Key caution: `publicly funded` or `state-owned` does not automatically mean democratically governed or technologically independent. Vendor dependence may remain.

## R3. Capability Building / Industrial Policy

Examples:
- R&D capability
- skills and research institutions
- procurement
- semiconductor or hardware capability
- compute capacity
- domestic/regional software/model capability
- public investment

Addresses primarily: L5 capability asymmetry and P2 dependency.

Key caution: buying equipment is not the same as building endogenous capability.

## R4. Open Ecosystems

Examples:
- open-source AI
- open weights
- open standards
- open hardware

Critical distinctions:

`open weights ≠ open source AI ≠ public infrastructure ≠ sovereignty`

Open access can reduce some dependencies while leaving chips, compute, data, deployment, expertise, and distribution concentrated.

## R5. Commons / Cooperative / Stewardship Governance

Examples:
- data commons
- data trusts / stewardship
- platform cooperatives
- governable stacks
- nonprofit or member-governed infrastructures

Key caution: `data trust` is not automatically a commons, and commons are not automatically Indigenous sovereignty.

## R6. Community / Indigenous Governance

Examples:
- collective authority over data use
- community-defined access and purpose
- CARE-type principles
- community-controlled language/data/model initiatives

This remains distinct from generic R5 because Indigenous sovereignty has a different normative and political genealogy.

## R7. Privacy-Preserving / Distributed Technical Architectures

Examples:
- federated learning
- privacy-preserving ML
- P2P/local architectures where appropriate
- distributed data processing

Key caution:

`technical decentralization ≠ democratic governance ≠ anti-monopoly`

These techniques should only be treated as sovereignty/dependency strategies when their system-level effect is demonstrated.

## R8. Resilience / Diversification / Managed Interdependence

Examples:
- supplier diversification
- regional pooling
- redundancy
- substitutability
- portability
- reducing single points of failure
- local/offline continuity where relevant

This family is important because full autarky is not the only alternative to dependency.

A useful later research question is whether the practical target should often be:

`exit capacity + substitutability + resilience + bargaining power`

rather than `full-stack self-sufficiency`.

# 6. C — Case / geopolitical-position overlay

`Global South` is not one causal actor. A country, region, or community can occupy several positions at once.

C1. **Data-source / extraction site**

C2. **Outsourced labor site**

C3. **Mineral / energy / ecological extraction site**

C4. **Linguistic / epistemic periphery**

C5. **Cloud / model / compute dependent market**

C6. **Standards / regulatory rule-taker**

C7. **Geopolitically coercible / vulnerable actor**

C8. **Regional technology power / intermediary hub**

C9. **Capability-building middle power**

C10. **Site of community / Indigenous / public alternatives**

### Why this matters

A country may have strong data localization but weak semiconductor capability; local cloud but frontier-model dependency; domestic models but foreign software/toolchain dependency; or strong technical capability but low influence in standards. Aggregate labels such as `sovereign` or `dependent` therefore hide more than they reveal unless the stack is specified.

# 7. Cross-map relations

## 7.1 Central analytical sequence

A useful causal sequence is:

`concentration → dependency → vulnerability → coercibility`

But these are **not synonyms**.

### Concentration
A small number of actors control a large share of assets or markets.

### Dependency
An actor relies on external capabilities, suppliers, standards, interfaces, or infrastructures.

### Vulnerability
A dependency is difficult or costly to substitute, absorb, or bypass.

### Coercibility
Another actor can exploit that vulnerability to alter behavior or deny capability.

### Sovereignty / autonomy
A proposed response concerning authority, capability, resilience, jurisdiction, ownership, or control.

The map therefore rejects this shortcut:

`market concentration → sovereignty`

The missing analytical middle is dependency/capability/vulnerability.

## 7.2 Coloniality and dependency are not synonyms

`coloniality` concerns historically durable structures of power, classification, knowledge, hierarchy, and modernity/coloniality.

`dependency` concerns asymmetric capability and structural reliance.

`weaponized interdependence` concerns strategic coercion made possible by network centrality.

The same empirical case may involve all three, but each mechanism must be shown separately.

## 7.3 Political economy and infrastructure are related but distinct

Political Economy asks:
- who owns?
- who captures value?
- who receives rents?
- how does corporate power reproduce itself?

Infrastructure/Materiality asks:
- what systems must exist for AI to function?
- where are bottlenecks and path dependencies?
- what physical/organizational resources are required?
- what fails when access is interrupted?

## 7.4 Sovereignty is not automatically emancipation

Reducing foreign dependence may:
- increase domestic capability and bargaining power;
- or merely transfer control from foreign firms to domestic state/security institutions.

Therefore every sovereignty proposal should be evaluated on at least:
- **who gains control?**
- **over what object?**
- **through what mechanism?**
- **with what rights/accountability?**
- **does it actually improve capability/resilience?**

## 7.5 Openness is not the same as control

Open weights or open-source software can increase access and exit options, but do not by themselves provide:
- compute
- chips
- data governance
- deployment infrastructure
- expertise
- financing
- democratic control

Likewise, public infrastructure may still depend on proprietary hardware or software.

# 8. Verified conceptual debates to preserve

## 8.1 Colonialism or capitalism?

This is a real analytical dispute. Colonial terminology can illuminate expropriation, externally imposed systems, racialized/territorial hierarchy, epistemic domination, and historically structured dependence. But not every instance of corporate concentration or profit-seeking requires a colonial frame.

**Rule for later research:** specify the colonial mechanism instead of using `colonialism` as a synonym for inequality.

## 8.2 Sovereignty or openness?

This is not a simple binary. Openness without capability can be hollow; sovereignty can become nationalism, censorship, or surveillance.

Better question:

**What combination of openness, capability, rights, substitutability, and governance produces meaningful autonomy?**

## 8.3 State sovereignty or community/Indigenous sovereignty?

State-centered sovereignty is only one model. Indigenous Data Sovereignty demonstrates a distinct conception grounded in collective self-determination and authority.

## 8.4 Open source or public infrastructure?

They are better treated as complements than substitutes. Open models may reduce API/model-layer dependency; public infrastructure may address compute/provisioning; neither alone solves the whole stack.

## 8.5 Regulation or ownership/provision?

Some literatures seek to constrain dominant firms through regulation/competition. Others ask whether critical infrastructures should be publicly or collectively provisioned. These are different intervention logics.

## 8.6 Decentralization or public centralization?

Expensive compute may remain physically centralized for scale/economics while governance and allocation can be plural, public, or community-accountable. Physical decentralization and political decentralization should not be conflated.

## 8.7 Open data or collective data authority?

Open-data/open-science goals can conflict with legitimate community control. CARE demonstrates why reuse and accessibility cannot be the only criteria.

# 9. Diagnosis versus alternatives

The working hypothesis survives verification in qualified form:

> Postcolonial/decolonial and data-colonial literatures are especially strong at diagnosing historical, epistemic, relational, and extractive dimensions of AI power. Concrete large-scale infrastructure and capability alternatives are more dispersed across public infrastructure, industrial policy, commons/cooperative governance, competition/interoperability, public administration, and sovereignty/strategic-autonomy literatures.

But this is **not** a claim that decolonial work has no alternatives.

Important counterexamples:
- Indigenous Data Sovereignty and CARE provide concrete governance principles.
- Postcolonial/Decolonial computing includes design and practice-oriented interventions.
- community/local-language initiatives can produce real technical artifacts.

The gap is most visible at the capital-intensive end of the stack: hyperscale compute, cloud, semiconductors, and large-scale industrial capability.

# 11. Important absences and limits of this map

This map settles conceptual structure. It does **not** yet establish volatile or case-specific empirical claims such as:

- current cloud or GPU market shares
- current capex comparisons
- exact geography and scale of RLHF/data-annotation labor
- exact tokenization cost penalties for Persian or other languages
- Gulf-state AI/compute hub claims
- comparative budgets of public/private compute programs
- whether SLMs, mesh networking, federated learning, RISC-V, or local inference materially create sovereignty in a specific case

These questions should be researched only when a later Thesisound axis requires them.

The map also does not presume that every technological dependency is undesirable. Future work should distinguish:
- normal specialization
- tolerable mutual interdependence
- asymmetric but substitutable dependence
- strategic vulnerability
- weaponizable chokepoint dependence
