# Thesisound Research Axes — AI Power, Coloniality, Dependency, Sovereignty, and Alternatives

Status: **research-axis map v1**
Date: 2026-08-17
Purpose: decompose the canonical field map into a small number of independent deep-research trajectories for future Thesisound work.

This file is **not** an episode list, **not** a production schedule, and **not** a reading list. Each axis is deliberately broader than an episode and should later be decomposed into narrower research questions, source packs, theses, and only then possible episodes.

Source of truth:
- `research/decolonial-ai/field-map.md`
- `research/decolonial-ai/RESEARCH_WORKFLOW.md`

## Design rule

Axes are not copies of the `L` literature nodes. Each axis is a research problem composed from:

`Literatures (L) × Sites (S) × Power mechanisms (P) × Responses (R) × Case positions (C)`

An axis deserves independent treatment when:
1. it asks a distinct causal question;
2. combining it with another axis would obscure a major conceptual distinction;
3. it requires a meaningfully different source family or empirical evidence base;
4. it can later generate multiple narrower Thesisound research questions.

The six axes below are therefore problem-oriented rather than disciplinary.

# Axis 1 — From Concentration to Dependency: The AI Capability Stack

## Core problem

How does concentration in semiconductors, accelerators, compute, cloud, software stacks, foundation models, and standards become **structural technological dependency** — and how is dependency different from ordinary specialization, market concentration, or simple foreign ownership?

This is the foundational material/capability axis of the project.

## Why it deserves an independent deep dive

The motivating problem cannot be analyzed accurately if all concentration is called dependency. A country or organization may have several vendors yet still lack meaningful substitutability because the vendors depend on the same accelerators, software ecosystem, cloud interfaces, standards, or upstream fabrication chain.

This axis is also where classical dependency/development thinking must be tested against the contemporary AI stack rather than merely invoked rhetorically.

## Scope

Primary sites:
- semiconductor design/fabrication/equipment/packaging
- accelerators and compute hardware
- data centers / HPC / compute capacity
- cloud infrastructure
- software frameworks and proprietary interfaces
- foundation models
- standards where they create lock-in or capability asymmetry

Primary mechanisms:
- concentration
- bottlenecks
- lock-in
- switching cost
- lack of endogenous capability
- dependence on upstream technical ecosystems
- limited substitutability / exit capacity

## Exclusions

Do not make this axis primarily about:
- data extraction or invisible labor — Axis 2
- linguistic/epistemic hierarchy — Axis 3
- active sanctions/export-control coercion — Axis 4
- normative sovereignty claims — Axis 5
- detailed institutional alternatives — Axis 6

Those may be referenced only where needed to define boundaries.

## Central research questions

1. What is the analytical difference among `concentration`, `dependency`, `vulnerability`, `coercibility`, and `capability`?
2. At which layers of the AI stack is dependency most difficult to substitute?
3. When does access to foreign technology build local capability, and when does it reproduce dependence?
4. Which dependencies are caused by market structure, which by accumulated knowledge/capability, and which by technical path dependence?
5. How useful are classical Dependency Theory and development/capability literatures for AI-era semiconductors, cloud, and models?
6. Is `exit capacity` / substitutability a more operational measure than nominal ownership or national branding?
7. How do standards, software ecosystems, and interfaces create dependence even without monopoly ownership?

## L/S/P/R/C mapping

**L:** L1 Political Economy; L3 Infrastructure/Materiality; L5 Dependency/Development/Capability; selected L6 IPE

**S:** S3–S8 and S13, with S1/S2 where resource/energy dependence is relevant

**P:** primarily P1 Economic/ownership and P2 Infrastructural/dependency

**R:** R1 interoperability/anti-lock-in, R3 capability building/industrial policy, R4 open ecosystems, R8 diversification/resilience

**C:** C5 cloud/model/compute-dependent market; C6 standards/rule-taker; C8 regional technology power; C9 capability-building middle power

## Likely source families

- political economy of platforms/AI
- infrastructure studies and cloud/AI industrialization
- semiconductor and supply-chain studies
- dependency/development economics
- innovation and technological-capability literature
- industrial policy
- standards and interoperability research

## Dependency on other axes

**Prerequisite for:** Axes 4, 5, and 6.

Axis 4 needs this baseline to distinguish dependency from active weaponization. Axis 5 needs it to define what sovereignty is responding to. Axis 6 needs it to evaluate whether proposed alternatives actually reduce system-level dependency.

# Axis 2 — Extraction and the Hidden Human Production of AI

## Core problem

How does AI depend on extraction of data, labor, and material resources, and under what conditions are concepts such as `Data Colonialism`, `Digital Colonialism`, `extractivism`, or global labor exploitation analytically more precise than generic critiques of capitalism?

This is the main extraction/labor axis.

## Why it deserves an independent deep dive

Data appropriation, microwork, annotation, content moderation, RLHF-related labor, mineral extraction, and ecological burdens are frequently narrated together as “AI extraction.” They are related, but they are not one mechanism. This axis should disaggregate them while testing where colonial/decolonial language adds explanatory value.

It also prevents the AI story from becoming an infrastructure-only account in which human labor disappears behind the word `automation`.

## Scope

Primary sites:
- data and data pipelines
- annotation/moderation/evaluation/RLHF-related labor
- platform/digital labor
- minerals and physical resource extraction where directly tied to AI supply chains
- ecological/resource burdens where distribution is central

Primary mechanisms:
- appropriation/enclosure
- labor exploitation and wage/value asymmetry
- outsourcing and invisibility
- unequal distribution of environmental/resource costs
- extraction of social life into data relations

## Exclusions

Do not treat:
- all low-resource-language problems as extraction — Axis 3
- all foreign infrastructure dependence as colonialism — Axis 1
- sanctions or denial of access as extraction — Axis 4
- every data-governance alternative as part of the diagnosis — Axis 6

## Central research questions

1. What exactly distinguishes `Data Colonialism` from surveillance capitalism, platform capitalism, and ordinary data extraction?
2. What distinguishes `Digital Colonialism` from Data Colonialism?
3. When is the colonial analogy historically/mechanistically justified, and when is `capitalism`, `labor exploitation`, `dependency`, or `infrastructural dominance` more precise?
4. What forms of human labor remain hidden behind AI systems, and how are value, risk, and visibility distributed?
5. How geographically concentrated are annotation, moderation, and post-training labor chains, and how variable are conditions across countries and firms?
6. How should material/mineral extraction be connected to digital/data extraction without treating them as identical?
7. Does the `Global South` category illuminate these chains, or does it conceal major differences among labor sites, resource exporters, digital markets, and regional powers?

## L/S/P/R/C mapping

**L:** L1 Political Economy; L2 Critical Data/STS; L3 Materiality/Political Ecology; L4 Postcolonial/Decolonial Technology; Data/Digital Colonialism interface; neighboring Digital Labor literature

**S:** S1/S2, S9, S10, selected S12

**P:** primarily P1 Economic/appropriative power; some P2 where supply-chain dependence matters

**R:** secondary links to R5 commons/stewardship, R6 community/Indigenous governance, labor regulation and procurement mechanisms

**C:** C1 data-source/extraction site; C2 outsourced labor site; C3 mineral/ecological extraction site

## Likely source families

- Data Colonialism / Digital Colonialism
- critical data studies
- digital labor / sociology of work
- labor-process and platform-work research
- political ecology / environmental justice
- decolonial technology studies
- empirical investigative/field studies of annotation and moderation labor

## Dependency on other axes

Can be researched in parallel with Axis 1.

Feeds **Axis 5** by showing why sovereignty cannot be reduced to infrastructure ownership, and feeds **Axis 6** by defining the problems that data/labor governance alternatives must address.

# Axis 3 — Epistemic Infrastructure: Language, Knowledge, Benchmarks, and Standards

## Core problem

How do AI systems make some languages, categories, forms of knowledge, evaluation criteria, and technical standards more legible or authoritative than others — and how does this create **epistemic/constitutive power** distinct from ownership or hardware dependence?

This is the field's epistemic and rule-setting axis.

## Why it deserves an independent deep dive

Language inequality, benchmark design, taxonomy/classification, training-data representation, and technical standard-setting are often scattered across Decolonial AI, Critical AI, NLP, sociolinguistics, and governance literatures. Keeping them only as a footnote to infrastructure would miss a distinct form of power: defining the categories through which systems represent and evaluate the world.

Standards also create a bridge between epistemic and infrastructural power: a default or protocol can shape interoperability and market access even when no actor directly owns the downstream application.

## Scope

Primary sites:
- language coverage and low-resource NLP
- tokenization where empirically consequential
- datasets as representations of knowledge/culture
- benchmarks and evaluation regimes
- taxonomies/classification systems
- standards, protocols, certification, technical defaults
- Indigenous/local knowledge where AI representation/governance is at stake

Primary mechanisms:
- classification and normalization
- epistemic hierarchy
- linguistic marginalization
- representation/legibility
- benchmark/default-setting
- standards-based lock-in and rule-setting

## Exclusions

Do not automatically label every model performance gap `coloniality`.

Do not turn this axis into:
- a general bias/fairness survey
- a generic NLP engineering survey
- a data-labor extraction axis — Axis 2
- an infrastructure-supply-chain analysis — Axis 1

## Central research questions

1. What does `epistemic coloniality` mean when applied to AI, and which mechanisms can be observed empirically?
2. When are language/model inequalities caused by data scarcity, tokenizer design, evaluation design, market incentives, or broader epistemic hierarchy?
3. What evidence exists for differential technical/economic burdens on low-resource languages, including but not limited to Persian?
4. How do benchmarks and evaluation standards encode assumptions about valid language, knowledge, behavior, or capability?
5. Who sets AI standards, and how do standards affect interoperability, lock-in, certification, and geopolitical influence?
6. How do Indigenous/community knowledge governance frameworks challenge default assumptions that more open data is always better?
7. When does standardization enable plural participation, and when does it universalize the categories of dominant actors?

## L/S/P/R/C mapping

**L:** L2 Critical Data/STS; L4 Postcolonial/Decolonial Technology; L8 Indigenous Data Sovereignty; neighboring sociolinguistics/NLP and standardization studies; selected L6/L7 for standards geopolitics

**S:** S9, S11, S13

**P:** primarily P4 Epistemic/constitutive; secondarily P2 dependency and P3a where standards become geopolitical instruments

**R:** R1 interoperability/open standards; R4 open ecosystems; R6 community/Indigenous governance; participatory/local-language model and evaluation practices within relevant families

**C:** C4 linguistic/epistemic periphery; C6 standards/rule-taker; C10 site of community/Indigenous alternatives

## Likely source families

- Decolonial AI / Postcolonial Computing
- Critical Data Studies
- sociolinguistics and low-resource NLP
- benchmark/evaluation studies
- Indigenous Data Sovereignty and CARE
- standardization studies / international AI standards governance

## Dependency on other axes

Can be researched in parallel with Axes 1 and 2.

Feeds **Axis 5** by broadening the meaning of sovereignty beyond hardware, and **Axis 6** by supplying requirements for community governance, open standards, local-language capacity, and participatory alternatives.

# Axis 4 — From Dependency to Coercion: Geoeconomics, Sanctions, and the Security–Technology Nexus

## Core problem

When does technological interdependence become **weaponizable political power**? How are chokepoints in chips, cloud, software, finance, standards, and platforms converted into sanctions, export controls, surveillance, denial of service, military capability, or strategic leverage?

This is the geopolitical/security axis closest to the original motivating concern.

## Why it deserves an independent deep dive

Dependency is not the same as coercion. A system can be highly dependent but never experience active denial; conversely, actors occupying central network positions can convert otherwise productive interdependence into strategic leverage under conflict.

This axis also allows analysis of the Big Tech–state–military nexus without incorrectly treating private firms as either autonomous market actors or simple state instruments.

## Scope

Primary sites:
- semiconductors and fabrication/tooling chokepoints
- accelerators/compute access
- cloud and software/service access
- standards and strategic rule-setting
- platforms and distribution where denial/surveillance matters
- military cloud / defense AI / dual-use infrastructures

Primary mechanisms:
- chokepoint effects
- panopticon/information effects where relevant
- export controls and sanctions
- denial of access
- strategic standards setting
- military/security integration
- internal administrative/security coercion when sovereignty policy expands domestic control

## Exclusions

Do not treat ordinary market dependence as weaponization.

Do not make this axis a generic military-AI ethics survey. The focus is infrastructure/network position and coercive capacity.

Do not fold the normative question “what should sovereignty mean?” into this axis — Axis 5.

## Central research questions

1. Under what network conditions does interdependence become weaponizable?
2. Which AI-stack chokepoints are actually usable for state coercion?
3. How do export controls, sanctions, service denial, and licensing interact with private technology firms?
4. How should the relation among Big Tech, national-security institutions, and military infrastructure be conceptualized: partnership, dependence, symbiosis, capture, or something else?
5. How do techno-blocs and strategic alliances change the topology of dependency?
6. Can diversification across multiple technology blocs reduce coercibility, or merely create poly-dependence?
7. What is the relation between external geopolitical coercion (P3a) and internal administrative/security coercion (P3b)?
8. How should cases such as Iran be studied without turning one national experience into the theory itself?

## L/S/P/R/C mapping

**L:** L3 Infrastructure; L5 Dependency/Capability; L6 IPE/Geoeconomics/Security; L7 Sovereignty/Strategic Autonomy; cross-cutting military-tech interface; selected L1 Political Economy

**S:** primarily S3–S8, S12, S13

**P:** P2 dependency, P3a external/geopolitical coercion, P3b internal administrative/security coercion; selected P1 corporate power

**R:** R1 anti-lock-in/interoperability, R3 capability building, R8 diversification/resilience, selected R2 public infrastructure

**C:** C5 dependent market; C6 rule-taker; C7 coercible/vulnerable actor; C8 regional power; C9 capability-building middle power

## Likely source families

- International Political Economy
- Weaponized Interdependence
- geoeconomics/economic statecraft
- semiconductor geopolitics
- sanctions/export-control studies
- cloud/infrastructure security studies
- military innovation / defense technology
- state–corporate political economy

## Dependency on other axes

**Requires Axis 1 conceptually.**

Axis 1 defines the dependency topology; Axis 4 asks when and how it becomes coercive.

Feeds Axis 5 by clarifying the actual threat models that sovereignty/strategic-autonomy claims purport to answer.

# Axis 5 — Sovereignty after Autarky: Control, Self-Determination, and Managed Interdependence

## Core problem

What should `digital`, `technological`, `data`, or `AI sovereignty` mean when full-stack self-sufficiency is unrealistic — and how can autonomy be pursued without converting foreign-dependency concerns into domestic state domination?

This is the conceptual bridge from diagnosis to response.

## Why it deserves an independent deep dive

`Sovereignty` is currently used to describe very different objects, actors, and goals. Without decomposition, the term can conceal contradictions:
- jurisdiction without capability;
- national infrastructure that still depends upstream on foreign vendors;
- localization that improves resilience but also expands surveillance;
- open access that reduces one dependency while leaving compute/hardware concentration untouched;
- state sovereignty that conflicts with Indigenous/community self-determination.

This axis is therefore not “why sovereignty is good.” It is a conceptual audit of what kinds of control are meaningful, legitimate, and feasible.

## Scope

Decompose sovereignty by:

### Object
- data
- cloud
- compute
- models
- software stack
- chips
- standards
- broader technical capability

### Actor
- state
- region
- public institution
- community
- Indigenous nation/people
- individual
- commons/cooperative institution

### Mode
- jurisdiction
- ownership
- operational control
- endogenous capability
- resilience
- substitutability / exit capacity
- self-determination

## Exclusions

Do not assume:
- national ownership = sovereignty
- localization = sovereignty
- open source = sovereignty
- sovereignty = autarky
- sovereignty = emancipation

Detailed institutional implementation belongs primarily to Axis 6.

## Central research questions

1. What are the meaningful differences among digital sovereignty, technological sovereignty, data sovereignty, strategic autonomy, AI sovereignty, and compute sovereignty?
2. Which forms of sovereignty are primarily legal/jurisdictional and which require productive/technical capability?
3. Is `strategic autonomy`, `resilience`, or `exit capacity` a better practical target than full sovereignty for many countries/institutions?
4. Which dependencies are tolerable because they are reciprocal/substitutable, and which become strategically dangerous?
5. How can sovereignty claims be evaluated against risks of censorship, surveillance, localization, and domestic monopoly?
6. How do Indigenous Data Sovereignty and community self-determination challenge state-centric frameworks?
7. What would a non-autarkic model of managed interdependence look like?
8. Can digital non-alignment / multi-alignment be framed rigorously without inventing a new doctrine unsupported by evidence?

## L/S/P/R/C mapping

**L:** L5 Dependency/Capability; L6 IPE/Security; L7 Sovereignty/Strategic Autonomy; L8 Indigenous Data Sovereignty; selected L4 decolonial/postcolonial literature

**S:** potentially all S layers, but each sovereignty claim must specify its object rather than invoke the whole stack generically

**P:** P2 dependency, P3a external coercion, P3b internal coercion, P4 epistemic power where community/knowledge self-determination is involved

**R:** R1–R8 as competing ways of operationalizing autonomy/control

**C:** C5–C10, especially dependent markets, rule-takers, coercible actors, regional powers, capability builders, and community alternatives

## Likely source families

- digital/data/technological sovereignty
- strategic autonomy
- dependency and development/capability
- international political economy
- Indigenous Data Sovereignty
- rights/civil-liberties critiques of localization and digital authoritarianism
- resilience and interoperability policy

## Dependency on other axes

**Requires conceptual outputs from Axes 1, 3, and 4.**

Axis 1 specifies what the actor depends on; Axis 3 shows that control also concerns knowledge/language/standards; Axis 4 specifies coercive threats.

Axis 5 then defines the evaluation criteria for Axis 6 alternatives.

# Axis 6 — Building Alternatives: Public Infrastructure, Commons, Openness, and Capability

## Core problem

Which technical and institutional arrangements can actually reduce concentrated power or dangerous dependency without reproducing either hyperscaler dominance or unaccountable state control?

This is the explicitly constructive axis.

## Why it deserves an independent deep dive

The diagnosis and solution literatures are fragmented. Public Compute, Public AI, industrial policy, open-source ecosystems, data governance, interoperability, regional pooling, cooperative governance, and Indigenous/community models come from different traditions and solve different problems.

A useful alternatives research program must therefore compare them by **problem addressed, layer affected, technical mechanism, institutional mechanism, maturity, residual dependencies, and governance risks** rather than treating them as one ideological package.

## Scope

Alternative families to compare:
- regulation / antitrust / anti-lock-in
- interoperability, portability, procurement rules, exit rights
- Public Compute / computing commons
- Public AI / public models, datasets, APIs, evaluation/deployment capacity
- public or regional cloud/compute pooling
- industrial policy and endogenous capability building
- open source / open weights / open standards / open hardware
- data commons / trusts / stewardship
- Indigenous/community data governance
- platform/cooperative governance
- privacy-preserving/distributed architectures where they solve a defined dependency/governance problem
- diversification, redundancy, and managed interdependence

## Exclusions

Do not treat a technical mechanism as politically emancipatory by default:
- federated learning ≠ decentralization of power
- open weights ≠ open source
- open source ≠ public infrastructure
- public ownership ≠ democratic governance
- RISC-V ≠ semiconductor sovereignty
- SLM/quantization/local inference ≠ system-level sovereignty
- mesh/offline-first ≠ model/compute independence

These are hypotheses to test at the relevant layer.

## Central research questions

1. Which alternative solves which specific mechanism of power or dependency?
2. Where is public provision superior to regulation, and where is regulation sufficient?
3. What does Public Compute change if hardware/software supply chains remain privately concentrated?
4. What does Public AI add beyond open-weight models?
5. When do open ecosystems materially improve exit capacity, and when do upstream dependencies remain unchanged?
6. Which governance forms protect communities from both corporate extraction and state appropriation?
7. What can CARE/Indigenous Data Governance contribute to foundation-model-era data governance without being diluted into generic `responsible AI`?
8. When is regional pooling more realistic than national self-sufficiency?
9. How should industrial policy distinguish purchasing infrastructure from building endogenous capability?
10. Which alternatives are mature institutions, which are current pilots, and which are mainly normative proposals?
11. What combinations are complementary rather than substitutes (for example open models + public compute + interoperability + community data governance)?

## L/S/P/R/C mapping

**L:** L7 Sovereignty; L8 Indigenous Data Sovereignty; L9 Response/Alternative umbrella; plus L1/L3/L5 to evaluate structural effects

**S:** potentially all layers, but most directly S3–S9, S12, S13

**P:** alternatives should be evaluated against P1–P4 rather than by ideological label

**R:** R1–R8 — this axis is the comparative analysis of the entire R overlay

**C:** C8–C10 plus C5/C6 where alternatives are designed for dependent/rule-taking actors

## Likely source families

- Public Compute / computing commons
- Public AI
- industrial policy / developmental state / innovation capability
- antitrust/interoperability/portability
- open-source/open-model governance
- commons/cooperative governance
- data trusts/stewardship
- Indigenous Data Sovereignty / CARE
- public administration and infrastructure governance
- regional digital infrastructure initiatives

## Dependency on other axes

**Synthesis axis.**

Requires problem definitions from Axes 1–5. Otherwise alternatives risk becoming technology solutionism or abstract institutional preference.

This should be researched after the diagnostic axes are sufficiently mature to specify what each intervention is supposed to fix.

# Cross-axis architecture

The six axes form a research dependency graph, not an episode sequence:

```text
Axis 1 — Capability stack / structural dependency
   ├──> Axis 4 — Weaponization / coercion
   ├──> Axis 5 — Sovereignty / managed interdependence
   └──> Axis 6 — Alternatives

Axis 2 — Extraction / labor
   ├──> Axis 5 — Sovereignty beyond infrastructure
   └──> Axis 6 — Data/labor governance alternatives

Axis 3 — Epistemic / language / standards
   ├──> Axis 5 — Knowledge/community/standards sovereignty
   └──> Axis 6 — Local-language, standards, community alternatives

Axis 4 — Coercion / security
   └──> Axis 5 — Threat model for strategic autonomy

Axis 5 — Sovereignty / managed interdependence
   └──> Axis 6 — Evaluation criteria for alternatives
```

## Recommended research order — not production order

A defensible research sequence is:

1. **Axis 1** — establish what dependency actually is.
2. **Axis 2 + Axis 3** — deepen the map beyond infrastructure into extraction, labor, language, knowledge, and rule-setting.
3. **Axis 4** — ask when those dependencies become coercive/security power.
4. **Axis 5** — evaluate sovereignty/autonomy only after the dependency and threat models are clear.
5. **Axis 6** — compare alternatives against the verified problems and trade-offs.

Axis 2 and Axis 3 can run in parallel because they address distinct mechanisms and evidence bases.

---

# Why six axes rather than the earlier topic clusters

The earlier agent outputs repeatedly grouped together topics that share a political theme but require different causal explanations. The final axes deliberately preserve the following separations:

- **concentration/dependency** vs **active coercion**
- **data/labor extraction** vs **epistemic/linguistic/standards power**
- **dependency diagnosis** vs **sovereignty as response vocabulary**
- **sovereignty concepts** vs **concrete institutional alternatives**
- **state control** vs **community/Indigenous self-determination**

These distinctions are necessary if future Thesisound episodes are to be evidence-grounded rather than built around attractive but overloaded labels such as `AI colonialism` or `AI sovereignty`.

# Step-5 conclusion

The canonical field map can be converted into six durable Thesisound research trajectories:

1. **AI capability stack and structural dependency**
2. **Data/resource extraction and hidden AI labor**
3. **Epistemic infrastructure: language, knowledge, benchmarks, and standards**
4. **Weaponized interdependence, coercion, and the security–technology nexus**
5. **Sovereignty, self-determination, and managed interdependence**
6. **Institutional and technical alternatives**

Together they preserve the project's core movement:

`power structure → dependency → extraction/epistemic hierarchy → coercion → sovereignty debate → alternative institution-building`

without pretending that these are one literature or one mechanism.
