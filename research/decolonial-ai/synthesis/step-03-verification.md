## Verification labels

- **VERIFIED WITH MODIFICATION** — the underlying field/claim is real, but Step 02's name, scope, or hierarchy needs refinement.

- **NOT VERIFIED** — targeted searches did not establish the claimed concept/status; do not use as canonical terminology without attribution.
- **SYNTHETIC LABEL** — useful internal wording may be retained only as our own analytical shorthand, never presented as an established literature term.

# 1. Verification of the proposed literature map (L)

## L1 — Political Economy of Digital / AI Capitalism

**Status: VERIFIED.**

There is a mature political-economy lineage dealing with platform monopoly, rents, intellectual monopoly, ownership of data/knowledge, corporate concentration, and infrastructural business power. It is not reducible to AI ethics or infrastructure studies.

Anchor evidence:
- Cecilia Rikap, *Capitalism, Power and Innovation: Intellectual Monopoly Capitalism Uncovered* (Routledge, 2021). The publisher description explicitly frames private control of knowledge/data as a source of rent and power and analyzes effects on developing economies.
  - https://www.routledge.com/Capitalism-Power-and-Innovation-Intellectual-Monopoly-Capitalism-Uncovered/Rikap/p/book/9780367750299
- Hao Qi, “Platform power: monopolisation and financialisation in the era of big tech,” *Cambridge Journal of Economics* 46(6), 2022, DOI 10.1093/cje/beac054.
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/cje/beac054
- Michael Kemmerling et al., “Digital power resources (DPR): the political economy of structural and infrastructural business power in digital(ized) capitalism,” *Socio-Economic Review* 21(4), 2023, DOI 10.1093/ser/mwac059.
  - https://doi.org/10.1093/ser/mwac059

**Decision:** retain L1 as first-order.

**Reason:** it asks a distinct causal question: who owns/control assets and knowledge, how accumulation/rents/monopoly are reproduced, and how corporate power is organized.

## L2 — Critical AI / Critical Data Studies / STS

**Status: VERIFIED WITH MODIFICATION.**

`Critical Data Studies` is a recognizable field label with an explicit genealogy beginning around 2014, while `Critical AI Studies` is newer and still described by current scholarship as an emerging field. STS is a much older established discipline that supplies methods and concepts to both.

Anchor evidence:
- Craig Dalton & Jim Thatcher, “What Does a Critical Data Studies Look Like, and Why Do We Care?” (2014) — early explicit call for Critical Data Studies.
  - https://www.societyandspace.org/articles/what-does-a-critical-data-studies-look-like-and-why-do-we-care
- Craig Dalton, Linnet Taylor & Jim Thatcher, “Critical Data Studies: A dialog on data and space,” *Big Data & Society* (2016), DOI 10.1177/2053951716648346.
  - https://doi.org/10.1177/2053951716648346
- Jonathan Roberge & Michael Castelle (eds.), *The Cultural Life of Machine Learning: An Incursion into Critical AI Studies* (Palgrave, 2020/2021), DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-56286-1.
  - https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-56286-1
- Roberge, Senneville & Morin, “How to translate artificial intelligence? Myths and justifications in public discourse,” *Big Data & Society* (2020), explicitly discusses the possibility of Critical AI Studies.
  - https://doi.org/10.1177/2053951720919968

**Decision:** retain L2, but name it in the canonical map as a **composite critical-sociotechnical family**, with internal maturity labels:
- STS — established discipline;
- Critical Data Studies — established subfield;
- Critical AI Studies — emerging field label.

**Reason:** treating all three as equally mature would overstate Critical AI Studies, while separating every one into a top-level node would make the map unnecessarily disciplinary rather than problem-oriented.

## L5 — Dependency / Development / Technological Capability

**Status: VERIFIED WITH MODIFICATION.**

Dependency theory and technological dependence are clearly independent historical lineages. Their direct application to contemporary AI is legitimate as an analytical bridge, but an integrated, mature canon called `AI dependency theory` was not established in this verification pass.

Anchor evidence:
- Theotonio Dos Santos, “The Structure of Dependence,” *American Economic Review* 60(2), 1970, pp. 231–236.
  - https://www.aeaweb.org/aer/top20/60.2.231-236.pdf

Dos Santos explicitly includes dependence on machinery, capital goods, and advanced technologies within structural dependence.

**Decision:** retain L5 because it supplies a mechanism that other nodes do not: reproduced asymmetry of productive/technological capability. However, canonical wording should distinguish:
- established historical dependency/development/capability literature;
- emerging application of that lens to AI/cloud/compute/model capability.

**Reason:** access to a technology is not the same as endogenous capability to reproduce, substitute, govern, or develop it. This is analytically different from monopoly, lock-in, or coercion.

## L7 — Digital / Technological / Data / AI Sovereignty & Strategic Autonomy

**Status: VERIFIED WITH MODIFICATION.**

Digital/data/technological sovereignty are established but contested policy-academic literatures. AI sovereignty and compute sovereignty are newer, rapidly growing, and conceptually less stable.

Anchor evidence:
- Anupam Chander & Haochen Sun (eds.), *Data Sovereignty: From the Digital Silk Road to the Return of the State* (Oxford University Press, 2023).
  - https://global.oup.com/academic/product/data-sovereignty-9780197582794
- Chander & Sun, “Digital Sovereignty as Double-Edged Sword,” in the above volume / earlier Virginia Journal of International Law version. The argument explicitly covers both citizen protection and expansion of state control.
  - https://academic.oup.com/book/55261/chapter/428625183
- `Digital/sovereignty and European security integration`, *European Security* (2022), connects digital/technological sovereignty with strategic autonomy and dependence reduction.
  - https://doi.org/10.1080/09662839.2022.2101886
- Ben Hawkins, Vili Lehdonvirta & Tony Wu, “AI Compute Sovereignty: Infrastructure Control Across Territories, Cloud Providers, and Accelerators” (SSRN working paper, 2026) — useful evidence of an emerging compute-sovereignty vocabulary, not a mature canon.
  - https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5367921

**Decision:** keep L7, but decompose internal terms and maturity:
- Digital sovereignty — established/contested;
- Data sovereignty — established/contested;
- Technological sovereignty / strategic autonomy — established policy/IR vocabulary;
- AI sovereignty — emerging and unstable;
- Compute sovereignty — emerging technical-policy sub-concept.

**Important verified warning:** sovereignty is not synonymous with emancipation. The academic literature directly recognizes that sovereignty claims can protect citizens while also enabling localization, surveillance, censorship, or state control.

# 8. Domestic surveillance / disciplinary power: P3 or new P5?

**Status: no evidence from this verification requires a new top-level P5.**

Digital-sovereignty scholarship directly documents the double-edged possibility that sovereignty can protect against external actors while expanding domestic state control, surveillance, censorship, or repression.

**Decision:** do not add P5. Refine P3 instead:
- **P3a external/geopolitical coercion** — denial, export controls, chokepoints, sanctions, military leverage;
- **P3b internal administrative/security coercion** — surveillance, policing, censorship, access control, state security uses.

Where systems also define who is legible/classified/normal, connect P3b to P4 epistemic/constitutive power.

**Reason:** the domestic/external distinction is important, but both remain coercive/security mechanisms. A fifth top-level mechanism would multiply categories by political scale rather than by mechanism.

# 9. Verification of key conceptual distinctions

## Concentration ≠ dependency
**VERIFIED as analytically necessary.** Political-economy sources study ownership/monopoly; infrastructure/dependency sources show that dependence may arise through technical stacks, switching costs, supplier concentration, or lack of endogenous capability.

## Dependency ≠ vulnerability
**Retain as project distinction.** A dependency becomes a vulnerability when disruption/denial would materially impair capability and substitution is costly/slow. This is a synthesis rule rather than a claim that one canonical AI literature defines it identically.

## Vulnerability ≠ coercibility
**Strongly supported by Weaponized Interdependence.** Network asymmetries can be weaponized when actors controlling hubs/chokepoints can exploit them for surveillance or denial.

## Sovereignty ≠ autarky
**VERIFIED as a necessary caution.** Contemporary sovereignty literature is contested and often concerned with control/capacity/strategic autonomy rather than literal self-sufficiency at every layer.

## Jurisdiction/localization ≠ capability
**VERIFIED as a necessary distinction.** Sovereignty work and compute/cloud literature repeatedly distinguish legal location/control from actual infrastructure/vendor/capability dependence.
